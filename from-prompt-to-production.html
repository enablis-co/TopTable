Step 02 · Frame it

Thin ticket in, assumptions out.

--- name: elaborator model: opus tools: Read, Grep, Glob, atlassian --- Pull the ticket. Follow the epic and every linked page before writing anything. 1. Review the acceptance criteria on the ticket. Say where they are thin, wrong, or contradict a linked page. 2. Elaborate them. Testable, one behaviour each, no implementation detail. 3. Produce a summary plan. Files, order, what you will not touch. 4. List separately: ASSUMPTIONS you had to make RISKS you cannot resolve from source Stop. Do not implement until a human has signed off the plan and both lists.

The criteria are the easy half. The valuable half is what it had to assume and what it could not find out.

An agent that lists its assumptions has told you exactly where it is about to go wrong. An agent that does not will pick a reading and commit.

Criteria Assumptions Risks Sign-off before code

The cheapest checkpoint in the process. Sixty seconds here saves forty minutes reviewing the wrong thing.

Step 03 · Choose the model

Plan on Opus, build on Sonnet.

Opus

Planning, ambiguous specs, architecture, review.

Sonnet

Implementation against a clear spec. Cheap enough to run three at once.

Haiku

Mechanical work. Renames, scaffolding, extraction.

--- name: test-author model: sonnet ---

The default is inherit. An Opus session spawns Opus subagents unless you say otherwise.

If you need Opus to implement, your spec is not finished.

Step 04 · Split it

Do not queue them. Fan them out.

One planning pass, then three jobs start together. The contract is the only thing that crosses lanes.

Step 05 · Delegate it

One job, one agent, one model.

spec-writer

opus

Elaborates the ticket and plans the work. Names the assumptions. Writes no code.

model-developer

sonnet

Domain and rules. Pure functions only. Cannot import a framework.

ui-developer

sonnet

Feature code. Renders from the violation list, never re-implements a rule.

test-author fixer refactorer migration-author and whatever your codebase needs
Dynamic, not a pipeline

The dispatch reads the ticket and picks the path. A rule change and a migration do not need the same sequence, and hardcoding one means every job gets the worst of both.

Effort is a dial

How hard it thinks is set per job, not per session. Planning wants all of it. A rename wants none, and paying for thinking on a rename is just slower.

An agent earns its place when it has a constraint you want enforced rather than remembered. Not when it sounds like a job title.

Step 06 · Agent review

Nothing reaches you that a machine could have caught.

model-developer→ verifiers→ findings→ model-developer→ re-verify→ you
Deterministic, and early

pre-commit

formatlintgitleaks

CI

typechecktestsdep audit

Table stakes. You already do this.

Verification agents

the judgement a linter cannot do

spec-auditor which acceptance criteria the diff does not cover

security-reviewer injection, secrets, authz on every new path

data-analyst query shape, cardinality, what breaks at scale

test-author tests from the criteria, never reads the implementation

Set up properly, this catches more than a human reviewer skimming three pull requests.

Step 07 · Human review

Shape, novelty, and who answers for it.

The agents settled whether it is correct. Criteria met, tests green, no secrets, lints clean. Do not spend your attention there, it was done better than you would do it.

Shape

Does this look like how we build things here, or like something that arrived from somewhere else?

New patterns

Has one been introduced, and did we want one? Three agents inventing three is how a codebase goes.

Point inspection

Line by line where the risk is. A scan where it is not. Two pairs of eyes on the first, one on the rest.

Where the risk is:

Data, money, auth The seams between two jobs Anything a verifier flagged and the builder waved off

The agents check whether it is correct. You check whether it is right, and you still answer for it.

Step 08 · Audit it

The summary goes back on the ticket.

Every commit

References the ticket key. That is the line-level trail and most teams already have it.

On merge

A build summary goes back on the ticket: the criteria that were agreed, the assumptions that were signed off, what shipped, what did not.

Commit messages tell you what changed. The summary tells you what was asked for, and the BA who wrote the thin ticket finds out what detail you needed.
